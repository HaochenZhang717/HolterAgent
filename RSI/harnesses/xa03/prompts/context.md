Reference knowledge (quoted from the sources):
Database and criteria
- Long-Term ST Database annotation classes (Jager et al., Med Biol Eng Comput 2003): axis shifts from body-position changes are "characterised by a change in the Q-, R- or S-wave amplitude"; intermittent bundle-branch block or other conduction changes "by bizarre and wider QRS complexes"; slow drifts come from heart-rate related diurnal changes or medication. Heart-rate related ST changes typically show "J point depression with positive slope; moving of T-wave into ST segment; T-wave peaking; or parallel shift of ST segment"; ischemic changes "horizontal flattening; down sloping; scooping; or elevation". The annotators separated heart-rate related from ischemic changes partly with the subject's clinical information, which you do not have; when the ECG evidence cannot separate them, say so.
- ACC/AHA 1999 ambulatory ECG guideline: ischemia is "flat or downsloping ST-segment depression >=0.1 mV, with a gradual onset and offset that lasts for a minimum period of 1 minute", episodes separated by at least 1 minute (5 minutes recommended). Left ventricular hypertrophy, pre-excitation, left bundle-branch block or intraventricular conduction delay >=0.10 s make ST unsuitable for ischemia detection; digoxin and some antidepressants distort the ST segment.
- The database measured ST at J+80 ms below 100 bpm, J+72 ms from 100 bpm, J+64 ms from 110 bpm and J+60 ms at 120 bpm or more (K21).

Heart rate: demand ischemia or heart-rate related change?
- Panza et al. (J Am Coll Cardiol 1992, K03): 89% of ischemic episodes during daily life were preceded by a heart-rate rise of >=10 bpm, largest in the 5 minutes before onset. The chance that a heart-rate rise led to ischemia grew with its size and duration, from 4% (5-9 bpm, under 10 min) to 60% (20 bpm or more, 40 min or more) (Andrews 1993, K04). A heart-rate rise therefore does not by itself make an episode non-ischemic.
- For ischemia (timing): peak heart rate came on average 80.7 s before the deepest ST depression, and in most episodes heart rate had returned to baseline while ST was still depressed (Quyyumi 1984, K01). On ambulatory ECG during walking, ST staying level until a heart-rate threshold and then falling steeply, or ST depression continuing while heart rate fell, marked ischemia with sensitivity 79.2% and specificity 94% (Ino-Oka 1996, K02).
- Against ischemia: 10 of 120 healthy volunteers had horizontal or downsloping ST depression of 1-2 mm on ambulatory ECG, usually during tachycardia (135 +/- 10.5 bpm) (Quyyumi 1983, K10). ST that recovers as soon as heart rate falls lacks the timing sign of K01/K02.
- No heart-rate rise: in one small study 35% of ischemic episodes had no heart-rate change or even a decline before onset (Campbell 1986, K08), and ST shifts at a normal heart rate support ischemia from coronary spasm (ESC 2019, K29). Without a preceding rise, demand ischemia is less typical (K03, K04) but ischemia is not excluded.

Shape and size
- Upsloping is not automatically benign. With J-point depression over 2 mm and ST still depressed 2 mm or more 0.08 s after J (slowly upsloping), 94% had coronary stenosis, similar to horizontal or downsloping patterns (Kurita 1977, K24); another exercise study found slow upsloping depression carried a low likelihood (perfusion ischemia 25%, versus 77% for horizontal or downsloping) (Desai 2002, K25). So judge the depth that persists late in the ST segment, not the slope alone.
- On 24-hour Holter in 63 healthy men, specificity was 1.0 for horizontal or downsloping depression over 0.20 mV and 0.95 for 0.15 mV or more (Vaage-Nilsen 1999, K26). With depression criteria of 0.05 mV instead of 0.1 mV, reader agreement was poor (kappa 0.32) and positional changes were found in 42% instead of 16% of patients (Jernberg 2002, K17).

Position, axis and QRS
- Against ischemia: "Positional changes characteristically have a very sudden onset and offset associated with motion artifact with no or little change in heart rate", often with taller R waves during the shift (Bjerregaard 2003, K14). Body-position change caused 36% and sudden QRS/ST voltage increase 26% of false-positive ST events in continuous monitoring (Drew 1998, K12).
- Size of the QRS change: in 21 healthy subjects on continuous vectorcardiography, body-position changes changed the QRS vector in 15 (71%) and the ST vector in 6 (29%), and the size of the positional QRS change was not related to the size of the ST change (R = .13, P = .57) (Norgaard 2000, K15). A small QRS change therefore does not exclude a positional ST shift, and a large one does not prove it.
- For ischemia: ischemic ST deviation developed gradually, over 15 to 20 minutes, to its maximum (Shusterman 2007, K16). Ischemia itself can raise R-wave voltage and shift the QRS axis toward the injured region (Schick 1980, K18), and lengthen the QRS and raise terminal QRS voltage in the lead(s) with the largest ST displacement, returning to baseline as the episode ends (Barnhill 1985, K19). Both were shown in transmural (spasm) ischemia.
- Opposite-sign deviations in two leads: attacks of vasospastic angina typically show ST elevation in leads corresponding to the culprit lesion and ST depression in contralateral leads (JCS 2013, K28).

Recurrence and baseline
- In patients with frequent ischemia on 72-hour Holter, heart rate at the onset of ST depression varied only 9% from day to day (Tzivoni 1987, K42); in the same individual, various episodes may be due to different mechanisms (Tzivoni 1996, K43).
- Correcting for ST deviation in a steady 5-minute period within 30 minutes before the event reduced Holter ischemic events from 16 to 3 of 66 patients (Bjerregaard 2003, K23); the database measured deviations from local references placed in non-ischemic data (Jager 2003, K22). Artifactual ST deviation often shows an abrupt J-point change that persists for hours without heart-rate change (Stone 2005, K36).

How to use this knowledge:
- Weigh the evidence; do not apply rules mechanically. Criteria and the typical features quoted above are evidence in either direction, never verdicts. When the evidence is mixed, express it as a probability. When a fact above decides your weighing, name its card id (Kxx) in the finding.
- Heart rate. ST changes that grow and recede with heart rate occur in heart-rate related change and in demand ischemia alike (most ischemic episodes in daily life follow a heart-rate rise, K03). A dissociation of one or two 15-s bins around a heart-rate step is not evidence either way, but the timing in K01/K02 is: ST that keeps deepening after heart rate has peaked (episode_dynamics hr_max_minus_st_peak_s negative = heart rate peaked first) or stays depressed for minutes while heart rate falls (st_trend) argues for ischemia; ST that falls and recovers in step with heart rate fits a heart-rate related change but does not exclude demand ischemia. What the ECG can add as well: an ST change per bpm far beyond this patient's response to other heart-rate surges outside ST episodes, or ST at a given heart rate far beyond the patient's usual level at that heart rate (rate_response), argues for ischemia; a response within the patient's usual range fits a heart-rate related change. If that is all the evidence there is, give a probability near the middle rather than deciding by rule.
- Position and conduction. A body-position or axis shift changes the Q-, R- or S-wave amplitude or shape in the same lead and at the same time as the ST change, which is then often abrupt (step-like; episode_dynamics max_single_step_frac near 1, morphology_steps st_steps or synchronous_st_and_qrs_steps). Because positional QRS and ST changes are not proportional (K15), an empty qrs_changed_leads in compare_morphology (a lead is flagged only at QRS correlation below 0.9, a peak-to-peak ratio outside 0.75-1.33 or a polarity flip) does not exclude position when the ST change itself is step-like with little heart-rate change (K14); look at the size and timing of any smaller QRS amplitude change in the same lead. In the other direction, an ST change that builds gradually over minutes with a stable QRS argues against position (K16), and a QRS change that develops gradually together with the ST change, in the lead with the largest ST deviation, can come from ischemia itself (K18, K19) and is not by itself proof of an axis shift. Opposite-sign ST changes in two leads occur in ischemia (K28) as well as in axis shifts; the onset (step-like or gradual) and the timing of any QRS change separate them. A conduction change widens the QRS.
- Recurrence. Several similar episodes in one record are expected in ischemia (K42) as well as in repeated positional or heart-rate related changes; recurrence alone does not decide the class, and each episode is judged on its own evidence (K43).
- Shape. Judge the shape of the change, not the absolute contour: compare the averaged beat during the episode with the same lead's beat before it. Above about 100 bpm the fixed J+80 point can lie on the T wave; use J+20 to J+60 and the image (K21). A shallow J-point depression with a positive slope whose ST is back near the pre-episode level by J+60 fits the heart-rate related pattern; a depression that is still deep at J+60 (or J+80 at lower rates) is not made benign by an upward slope (K24, K25).
- Context. Candidate boundaries come from a threshold detector on deviations from a record-wide reference. Check (episode_context) whether the candidate is a fragment of a longer change, the return between two episodes, or a change of a lead whose usual level is far from zero, and judge the change itself (extremum minus the local level before it, and the absolute level; K22, K23).
- ST elevation can be ischemic (Long-Term ST Database classes; COVADIS criteria for vasospastic angina, Beltrame et al., Circ J 2016: transient "ST segment elevation >=0.1 mV"). A transient elevation that rises and falls smoothly with a stable QRS is positive evidence; a step-like shift at a heart-rate or posture change is not.
- Leads: a record may have only 2 or 3 leads with non-standard names; rules for contiguous 12-lead groups do not apply there.
